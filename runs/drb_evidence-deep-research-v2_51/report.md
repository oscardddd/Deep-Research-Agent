# Japan Elderly Population and Consumption Market Analysis 2020–2050

**Question:** From 2020 to 2050, how many elderly people will there be in Japan? What is their consumption potential across various aspects such as clothing, food, housing, and transportation? Based on population projections, elderly consumer willingness, and potential changes in their consumption habits, please produce a market size analysis report for the elderly demographic.

## Direct answer

Japan's elderly (65+) population is projected to peak at about 39.53 million in 2043, up from 36.243 million in 2024 and 28.6% of the population in 2020, before slightly declining toward 2050 [1, 2].

Elderly consumption patterns shift with age: shares on food, utilities, and medical care rise, while housing and clothing decline. Non-working elderly households exhibit average propensity to consume over 115%, financing deficits by dissaving [3, 4].

Consumer willingness among the elderly is characterized by high consumption relative to income, growing reliance on dissaving, and increasing demand for long-term care. The 'silver market' is expected to expand, driving innovation in healthcare, housing, and leisure [5, 6, 3].

## Executive summary

- The elderly population in Japan is projected to increase from 28.6% of total in 2020 to a peak of 39.53 million in 2043, reaching about 35% by 2050 [1, 7].
- Elderly households spend disproportionately on food, utilities, and medical care, while housing and clothing shares decline with age. Non-working elderly households had an average propensity to consume of 115.8% in 2023 [3, 4].
- The silver market is expected to grow, driven by population aging and changes in consumption habits, but overall aggregate consumption may decline due to lower average spending per elderly person [5, 8].

## Findings

### Elderly Population Projections 2020–2050

The percentage of the population aged 65 and over is expected to rise from 28.6% in 2020 to 38.7% in 2070 [1].

The size of the population aged 65 and over peaks at 39.53 million in 2043 [1].

As of October 1, 2024, the elderly population was 36.243 million (29.3% of total) [2].

By 2050, the share of the population aged over 60 is expected to exceed 40% [7].

### Elderly Consumption Patterns: Clothing, Food, Housing, Transportation

Elderly one-person households spend larger shares on food, utilities, household goods, and medical care, while spending on housing and clothing decreases with age [3].

In 2022, non-working elderly households (two-or-more-person, head aged 60+) allocated 29.5% to food, 6.9% to housing, 11.9% to transportation & communication. Clothing was not separately listed in the truncated excerpt [4].

Non-working elderly households have an average propensity to consume over 100%, with a deficit of disposable income to consumption expenditures of 34,642 yen in 2023, financed by withdrawing financial assets [9, 3].

Age-profile analysis shows that food and transportation follow a hump-shaped pattern, clothing declines monotonically, while medical care and utilities increase over the life cycle [8].

Japanese elderly households barely consume out of real assets, with a dissaving rate from real assets of only 0.7% in 1999, much lower than U.S. elderly [10].

### Consumer Willingness and Habit Changes

The increasing number of elderly will drive the 'silver market', boosting demand in healthcare, financial services, housing, and leisure [5].

The deficit of disposable income to consumption expenditures for non-working elderly households increased from 26,233 yen in 2022 to 34,642 yen in 2023, indicating growing reliance on dissaving [3].

The share of formal long-term care in GDP tripled from 0.7% in 2000 to 2.1% in 2019, reflecting increasing demand for care services [6].

In an aging economy, aggregate consumption may decline more sharply than the population itself, as the elderly have lower average consumption [8].

## Limitations

- Projected elderly population numbers and percentages for the full 2020-2050 period are missing.
- Projections for elderly sub-groups (e.g., by household type or age band) are not provided.
- Official projection sources beyond the single provided evidence are lacking.
- Consumption potential data (clothing, food, housing, transportation) for the elderly is absent.
- Evidence on elderly consumer willingness and consumption habit changes is missing.
- Comparison data between elderly and non-elderly consumption is not available.
- Detailed age-specific population projections for 2020–2050 at 5-year intervals were not fully available; peak year (2043) and 2020/2070 percentages are reported.
- Clothing expenditure data for elderly households is partially missing in the evidence; only directional trends are available.
- Some evidence on consumption patterns is from 1999 or 2022 and may not reflect current trends.
- Quantitative market size estimates are not directly provided; the report relies on qualitative consumption shifts and aggregate economic interpretations.
